<section class="lightning" data-transition="fade" aria-label="Remixing Plato assignment and student feedback">
  <p class="eyebrow">Critical Thinking with AI</p>
  <h2 class="main-point">Remixing Plato</h2>
  <p class="assignment-summary">Students use <i>Phaedrus</i> to create a dialogue<br>about AI, then refine its competing perspectives.</p>
  <p class="assignment-summary">Students define the characters' positions<br>and decide which perspectives to develop.</p>
  <div class="feedback">
    <span class="label">Student feedback</span>
    <p>They had not seen AI's potential to help them<br><strong>explore new perspectives.</strong></p>
  </div>
  <aside class="notes">
    <p><strong>0:20–1:00 (40 seconds)</strong></p>
    <p>In the Plato remix, students use Phaedrus as a model for a contemporary dialogue about AI. They define characters with different positions, ask AI to draft their exchange, and revise it to sharpen those perspectives. They decide which missing viewpoints would make the conversation more interesting. The response was overwhelmingly positive. Students had not seen AI's potential to help them explore new perspectives. Directing and revising the dialogue gave them a way to experiment with that possibility while keeping their own judgment central.</p>
    <p class="sources">Sources: <a href="https://fredgibbs.net/courses/critical-thinking-with-ai/dialogue-remix">Dialogue remix</a>, Assign roles, Draft, Revise and Iterate, and Refine; classroom feedback supplied by Fred Gibbs for this talk.</p>
  </aside>
</section>

<section class="lightning" data-transition="fade" aria-label="Complicate the Obvious assignment">
  <p class="eyebrow">Diet, Health, Expertise</p>
  <h2 class="main-point">Complicate the obvious</h2>
  <p class="prompt">“What is a healthy diet?”</p>
  <p class="assignment-summary">Students use course readings to question<br>the answer's assumptions and omissions.</p>
  <div class="feedback">
    <span class="label">Student feedback</span>
    <p>A better understanding of <strong>generic responses</strong><br>and <strong>silences</strong>: what an answer leaves out.</p>
  </div>
  <aside class="notes">
    <p><strong>1:00–1:40 (40 seconds)</strong></p>
    <p>Complicate the Obvious begins with a familiar question: what is a healthy diet? Students use course readings to investigate the AI answer's assumptions and omissions, then develop follow-up questions and their own analysis. Again, the response was overwhelmingly positive. Students came away with a better understanding of generic responses and silences: what an answer leaves out even when it sounds reasonable. Their historical knowledge gave them specific ways to question that answer and see why the omissions matter.</p>
    <p class="sources">Sources: <a href="https://fredgibbs.net/courses/diet-health-expertise/healthy-diet-analysis">Complicate the Obvious</a>, Required prompts, Questions to consider, and What to submit; classroom feedback supplied by Fred Gibbs for this talk.</p>
  </aside>
</section>

<section class="lightning" data-transition="fade" aria-label="Fast food class discussion and feedback">
  <p class="eyebrow">History of American Food</p>
  <h2 class="main-point">Discussing fast food with AI</h2>
  <p class="assignment-summary">Students compare AI summaries with the article.</p>
  <p class="discussion-question">What did AI do with the text?<br>What did it miss?</p>
  <div class="feedback">
    <span class="label">Classroom feedback</span>
    <p><strong>Very lively engagement.</strong> My sense was that<br>students learned more than in our usual discussion.</p>
  </div>
  <p class="context">It also helped me get out of my own discussion rut.<br>Reading: Steven Penfold, “Fast food”</p>
  <aside class="notes">
    <p><strong>1:40–2:25 (45 seconds)</strong></p>
    <p>For the fast-food article in American food history, students brought an AI summary and also skimmed the text themselves. We framed the class conversation around what AI did and did not do with the reading. The engagement was very lively. My sense was that students learned more than they would have in a regular discussion of the article. Part of that was getting out of my own discussion rut. Comparing AI's treatment with the text gave us another way into a reading I might otherwise have discussed in familiar ways.</p>
    <p class="sources">Sources: <a href="https://fredgibbs.net/courses/american-food/schedule">History of American Food, November 18: Fast food frameworks</a>, assigning Steven Penfold, “Fast food,” <i>The Oxford Handbook of Food History</i>, pp. 279–302; classroom observations and the comparison with a usual discussion supplied by Fred Gibbs for this talk.</p>
  </aside>
</section>

    <p>These observations come from American food history, critical thinking with AI, and diet, health, and expertise. Many students had little practice using AI productively. Many were skeptical and wanted a say in its use. The feedback was overwhelmingly positive about getting help to explore what AI could contribute to their learning.</p>

<section class="lightning" data-transition="fade" aria-label="Purpose and agency">
  <p class="eyebrow">Teaching approach</p>
  <h2 class="main-point">Purpose and agency</h2>
  <div class="principle">
    <h3>Transparency</h3>
    <p>Every assignment needs an explicit purpose<br>and support for doing the work.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="principle">
    <h3>Agency</h3>
    <p>Students need practice making decisions<br>about how AI helps them learn.</p>
  </div>
  <p class="learning">AI can make students more critical and more capable.</p>
  <aside class="notes">
    <p><strong>3:10–3:35 (25 seconds)</strong></p>
    <p>For me, this comes down to transparency and agency. I need to explain why an assignment matters and provide support for doing it. Students need practice making decisions about AI, including when to go beyond it. The work should make their own thinking visible. Done well, this leaves students more critical and more capable.</p>
    <p class="sources">Source: Fred Gibbs, AI assignment feedback, My approach / philosophy.</p>
  </aside>
