## Course Description
What makes someone an expert? And why does anyone believe them?

Every scholar depends on knowledge they can't check for themselves: a colleague's archival work, a translation, an edition, a dataset, a footnote. Expertise is how we manage that dependence, and trust is what makes it work. Neither is natural or permanent. Both have been built over time by people, processes, and institutions, often in response to a new technology that disrupted old arrangements.

Generative AI is the latest of these technologies, and it is routinely called unprecedented. This seminar argues that it isn't, and that historians are especially well equipped to say why. Plato worried that writing would give people the appearance of wisdom without the substance. Early print multiplied errors as fast as it multiplied books, and it took generations of correctors, privileges, and registers before readers trusted a printed page. Experimental science had to recruit credible witnesses before anyone believed a demonstration. Statistics, mechanical calculation, and keyword search each promised to replace expert judgment, and each ended up relocating it. **In every case, trust did not arrive with the technology. People had to manufacture it.**

The course moves in three parts. First we build a working vocabulary for expertise and trust from philosophy, sociology, and the history of science. Then we work through historical disruptions of expertise, from medieval charters to digitized newspapers. Finally we turn to AI itself: what language models actually do, how their interfaces perform authority, and what happens to scholarly expertise when anyone can produce in seconds something that looks like a historian's work.


{% include alert.html class='warning' title='Driving Question' text="
If a machine can produce something that looks like expertise, what was expertise in the first place?
"%}


## Learning Outcomes
* Explain key frameworks for thinking about expertise and trust from philosophy, sociology, and the history of science
* Use historical cases, from manuscripts to search engines, to analyze how new technologies have disrupted, relocated, and rebuilt expertise
* Evaluate claims about AI, both utopian and apocalyptic, against historical precedent rather than against the word "unprecedented"
* Assess how AI handles your own field: where it's reliable, where it fails, and how you would know
* Articulate what your own expertise consists of and how to make it legible to others


## Four Problems
Four questions run through every week of the course, whether the expert in question is a medieval scribe, a Victorian statistician, or a chatbot.

* **Definition:** What makes someone (or something) an expert?
* **Identification:** How can a non-expert tell a reliable expert from a merely confident one?
* **Deference:** When is it rational to set aside your own judgment for someone else's?
* **Transfer:** How does knowledge actually move from an expert to anyone else?


## You are welcome here
This seminar is designed for history graduate students, and students from every discipline are welcome. **You don't need any technical background, and you don't need to like technology.** We build the few technical ideas we need from the ground up, and only as far as they help with historical and epistemological questions.

Skepticism about AI is an asset here, as long as you can explain it. What you do need is a field you care about: the best test of anything we read is what it says about expertise you already have.

## Course Work
These are draft assignments and weights.

* Seminar participation and discussion leading (20%)
* Weekly reading responses (20%)
* Field Audit (15%)
* Trust Technologies, Then and Now (15%)
* Final project (30%)

### Seminar participation and discussion leading
Come having read, and come ready to talk. Each of you will also co-lead discussion once or twice during the semester: frame the week's readings, put them in conversation with earlier weeks, and bring the questions you think are worth our time.

### Weekly reading responses
A short response (~500 words) posted before each seminar. Don't summarize. Put the week's readings in conversation with each other or with earlier weeks, and end with a few questions you want to discuss.

### Field Audit
Choose a live debate in a field you know well. Ask an AI system the kinds of questions you'd expect on a comprehensive exam, and check everything it tells you, especially the citations. Then try the same thing in a field you don't know. Write up (~2,500 words) where the system was reliable, where it failed, what its failures reveal about the field's literature, and how you could tell. The comparison between your two fields is the point: what could you catch, and why?

### Trust Technologies, Then and Now
Choose a historical technology of trust from the course (the witness list, the colophon, the footnote, the referee report, the credential, the published table) and a feature of an AI interface that does similar work (cited sources, confidence language, visible "reasoning," personas, disclaimers). Analyze how each asks to be trusted and what it offers in return (~2,000 words). Optionally, add a short design brief for an interface that earns trust rather than performing it.

### Final project
Choose one:
* **Seminar paper** (~6,000--7,500 words) on a historical case of a technology disrupting expertise, with an explicit argument about what it does and doesn't tell us about AI.
* **Research protocol** for your own research: the written rules for how you read, take notes, and synthesize with AI assistance; the files the process leaves behind; and an essay (~4,000 words) on what writing your method down revealed about your expertise.

Everyone presents their project to the seminar at the end of the semester.

## AI Policy & Academic Integrity
* AI is one of the things this course studies, so we use it often, deliberately, and critically. Each assignment says what kind of AI use is expected. Sometimes that's none at all; sometimes the AI is the object of the assignment.
* **Show your checking.** Whenever you use AI in your coursework, say briefly what you used it for and how you verified what it gave you. That's not a confession. It's the practice this course is about: making judgment visible so that others can trust it.
* **Differentiate yourself from AI.** If your work resembles vanilla AI (_even if you wrote it by hand_), you will need to revise it for credit.

You should be familiar with [UNM's Policy on Academic Dishonesty and the Student Code of Conduct](https://pathfinder.unm.edu/code-of-conduct.html), which outline academic misconduct defined as plagiarism, cheating, fabrication, or facilitating any such act.
