# The 1619 Project Discussion Post

Historical debates don't live only in the classroom. 

The 1619 Project took a historiographical argument and put it in a newspaper, then a curriculum, then a political fight — and the controversy that followed wasn't really about the past so much as how the past gets used. 

This post asks you to do two things: judge the argument on its own terms, and place it in the long story of historiography this course has been tracing all semester.

{% include alert.html class='success' title='Skills you are practicing' text="
* **Argument analysis** — evaluating how Hannah-Jones builds her case and whether the evidence supports it, rather than just reacting to the conclusion.
* **Structural analysis** — asking why a debate over historical interpretation escalated into a fight over school curricula and political identity, not just who has the facts right.
* **Connecting frameworks across the semester** — placing a current debate inside the longer arc of how historians have argued about truth, evidence, and authority.

## What to write

Write a **discussion board post of about 800 words** that addresses both questions below — roughly half your space for each.

### 1. What is YOUR take on the pros and cons of what the 1619 Project is trying to do?

Address both sides fairly, regardless of your own view of the project. What does it get right — as an argument, a historiographical act, a public intervention? Where does it not make sense or seem wrong? 

### 2. How does this debate fit into the long history of historiography we've been studying?

Where does the 1619 Project sit in the arc of the course? Which historiographical frameworks or thinkers we've read does it resemble, challenge, or extend? Ground your argument in specific readings from across the semester.

### Somewhere in the post, land it on something real

At some point — it can be a few sentences, it doesn't need its own section — connect this debate to something in your own life. The history you were taught in high school and how it handled slavery. An argument at a family dinner. A statue, a street name, a school named after someone. A social media fight you scrolled past. A textbook you still remember.

This is not decoration to your "main" post. The 1619 Project's central claim is that the story a country tells about its founding shapes the country — so the test of that claim runs through the version of the story *you* were handed.

## A note on AI

Of course AI handle the summary part of the assignment easily, and if you put some effort in, you can get AI to provide the course connections as well. The 1619 debate is thoroughly documented online, and any chatbot will hand you a balanced, competent, completely generic 800 words in a few seconds.

That is exactly why it's worth almost nothing here. 

**Write this one yourself, but limited AI use is OK for additional research, polishing, or proofreading.** It should not write anything for you; it can fix typos.

## What I'm looking for

There's no right answer to either question. But vague generalizations unsupported by the readings won't earn full credit. The more specifically you draw on the texts — Hannah-Jones alongside Trouillot, or Lowenthal alongside Ranke — the stronger your post will be.

- **A** — both questions argued with specifics, not just asserted; pros and cons are genuinely weighed rather than picking a side and defending it; the historiographical connection draws on readings from across the semester, not just this week; lands the debate on something concrete from your own experience
- **B** — both questions addressed with reasonable specificity; draws on more than one reading; the connection to the rest of the course is present but could be sharper; some real-world anchor, even if brief
- **C** — addresses the prompts but stays general; little direct engagement with the assigned texts; the historiographical connection is thin or generic; no trace of your own encounter with this history
- **D** — one-sided or vague; skips one of the two questions; little evidence of having engaged with the readings
- **F** — minimal effort, or a generic summary of the controversy that names no course readings and could have been written by anyone
