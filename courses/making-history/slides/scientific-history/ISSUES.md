## 1. Humboldt is paraphrased, not quoted — needs the real text

**Status:** open. Needs a copy of the reading.

The only words attributed to Humboldt anywhere in the deck are his opening
sentence, "The historian's task is to present what actually happened," cited
to p. 57. That page is right — it is where the essay begins in *History and
Theory* 6, no. 1 (1967): 57–71, the translation the schedule assigns — and the
sentence is confirmed independently, including verbatim on the schedule line
itself.

Everything else the deck says about Humboldt is **paraphrase in my own voice**,
because the text could not be obtained: `~/Dropbox/zotero/von Humboldt/1967/
von_Humboldt-On_the_Historian's_Task.pdf` is a **0-byte stub**, and the
*History and Theory* text is not otherwise reachable. The slide "He opens with
the promise, then spends the essay taking it apart" therefore paraphrases
three claims — that an event is only partly available to the senses; that
sorting facts yields a skeleton, not an event; that the historian must show
each event as part of a whole.

**What to do:** get the PDF (JSTOR, or ILL), then replace the paraphrase with
two real quotations and page numbers. The two worth hunting for are the
"only partially visible to the world of the senses" passage and the "skeleton
of an event" passage — both are widely quoted and both are load-bearing here,
since the whole point of the slide is that Humboldt undercuts his own first
sentence.

Related, and already handled: the deck dates the essay "delivered 1821,
published 1822" on the title slide, which resolves the clash between Popkin
(1821) and the schedule (1822). No action needed.

## 2. Humboldt portrait carries no date

**Status:** open, low priority. Documented in `images/README.md`.

Wikimedia Commons records no date for the Lawrence portrait (RCIN 404936), and
rct.uk refuses automated requests, so the date could not be confirmed against
the holding institution. Secondary sources put the sitting around 1828 and say
Lawrence left it unfinished at his death in 1830, with the canvas completed by
Richard Evans — plausible but unverified, so **none of it is asserted in the
caption**. If you can reach the RCT record, add the date.
