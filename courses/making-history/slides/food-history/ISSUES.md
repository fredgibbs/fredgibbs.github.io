# Open issues — The History of Food (15.1)

Contributor notes, excluded from the build. Deliberate deviations are recorded
here so a later review does not raise them again.

## The slide contract this deck is built on

**A historiographic arc, then Thanksgiving taken apart.** The brief
(2026-11-22) asked for an overview of what the history of food can teach,
modeled on the history-of-science and history-of-medicine decks and drawing
on the American Food course (HIST 413). The author chose a historiographic
arc narrated by Belasco, with Siskind's essay and then a dish-by-dish payoff
after it. The headlines read in order:

1. **Cold open.** Ferris's *First Thanksgiving 1621* (about 1915), with an
   image-notes slide on three of its anachronisms.
2. **Part one, food as material (1960s–1990s).** Fabre's complaint that
   history passed over the fields; Braudel and daily bread, Mintz and sugar,
   Cronon and the packers. Historians did study food, as supply, labor and
   trade.
3. **Part two, what food meant (Belasco pp. 2–4).** On 2026-11-24 the author
   moved Belasco's reasons slide after the survey: the point is not that no
   one studied food, which part one shows they did, but that scholars avoided
   its meanings ("the wider meaning of our food behaviors," p. 2), which the
   reappraisals of the 1980s–90s took up. Then Shapiro on women's food work,
   the American-food cards (HIST 413) and the 1917 poster.
4. **Part three, the triangle (Belasco pp. 7–11).** Identity, convenience,
   responsibility; a discussion slide on why responsibility sits on top.
5. **Part four, Siskind.** Thanksgiving's present form dates from the late
   1800s; 1621 was not a Puritan thanksgiving; early thanksgivings celebrated
   victories over Native peoples; the feast moved into the family home; the
   South joined as Reconstruction ended; the Pilgrims entered the story when
   immigrants had to be made American (Yezierska); a discussion slide on the
   *Smithsonian* menu.
6. **Part five, the table.** Turkey (twice), pumpkin pie, sweet potatoes,
   cranberry sauce, each older than the holiday.
7. **Arc, take home, next.** The closing question repeats the week's
   reading-refraction prompt.

## Decisions

**The American-food cards slide is a condensation, not a reading.** Its six
claims paraphrase the HIST 413 readings named in its `.cite` line (Stavely
and Fitzgerald, Snell, Barbas, Biltekoff, Veit, Specht, Rude, Penfold,
Belasco). Several of those PDFs are Dropbox online-only placeholders and were
not reopened; the cards rest on the course's own framing. Check them against
the readings before relying on any one in discussion.

**One detail runs over the 30-word target** and stays under the 40-word stop:
the turkey slide (32 words), which carries two page citations.

**Belasco's spaced en dashes** are kept as printed (pp. 2 and 8, checked
against the page images); pdftotext renders them as hyphens.

**Belasco's quotations sit under other scholars' headlines** on the Annales
and Mintz slides. Belasco does not discuss Braudel or Mintz; his p. 1 and p. 2
sentences state the claim each of them demonstrated, and the cite names him.
The fresh-context review flagged that students may read them as Belasco's
verdict on those books. Kept, because the narrator's voice is what ties part
two together; swap in a line from Braudel or Mintz if that reading becomes a
problem in class.

**Braudel's book** is given as *Capitalism and Material Life* (1967, English
1973), the book that opens with population and daily bread. *The Structures
of Everyday Life* is its 1979 revision.

**"Older than the holiday"** means older than the holiday's present form,
which Siskind dates to the end of the 19th century. The foods are older (all
four are in Simmons, 1796); some of their present forms (canned sauce, 1912;
the marshmallow topping, 1917) are not, which is what the dish slides show.

## Fresh-context review, 2026-11-22

An agent with no memory of the drafting checked every quotation against the
cached texts and page images. All the quotations matched. Fixed from its report:
"two short documents from 1621" (only Winslow's letter is from 1621; Bradford's
account was finished in 1650); the "older than the holiday" claim, narrowed as
above; Braudel's title; Libby's date; the turkey detail's page citations
(Europe is p. 179, not p. 177); the arc card that credited home economics,
which the deck had treated as part of the neglect; the South's refusal, which
now gives Siskind's main reason (Reconstruction's citizenship for freed
slaves) rather than her partial one (abolitionist sermons); the HIST 413 cite
(now names the course); the Simmons quotation (now marked as cut off); the
1858 detail (pp. 175–76); the quincentenary citation (p. 186); and the
take home on the moralist, since Belasco sets himself apart from "the
moralist" (p. 8). Not changed: "Jean-Henri Fabre" is the standard spelling
(Belasco prints "Jean Henry"); Simmons's "Potatoes" probably means white
potatoes, but the quotation is there for "Yams."

## Open questions

**Yezierska's date.** Siskind dates "America and I" to 1903 (p. 183); the
essay was published in 1923 (in *Children of Loneliness*), and the deck says
1923.

**Quotations taken through Siskind or Belasco** were checked against their
pages, not against the originals: Increase Mather (1676), Appelbaum (*Thanksgiving:
An American Holiday, An American History*, 1984) on Alabama in 1875 and on the
chicken pie, Hobsbawm (1983), Yezierska, and Cronon (*Nature's Metropolis*,
1991). Each cite says "quoted in."

**Simmons is cited by recipe, not page.** The quotations come from the
Internet Archive transcription of the Hartford 1796 first edition
(`americancookery12815gut`), which has no page numbers.

**Facts from outside the readings,** sourced from secondary or web sources
rather than the assigned texts:
- turkey domesticated twice, in Mexico and the American Southwest (Speller et
  al., *PNAS* 2010);
- the Broad Breasted White cannot mate naturally;
- Marcus Urann's canned cranberry sauce, 1912;
- the cranberry scare of 9 November 1959 (aminotriazole; Secretary Flemming,
  HEW);
- the 1917 Angelus Marshmallows recipe booklet (by Janet McKenzie Hill) as
  the usual origin of the marshmallow topping — widely repeated, booklet not
  seen;
- Libby's pumpkin-pie recipe on the can, 1950 (Libby's own history; 1929, sometimes given, is when Libby's bought the Dickinson canning business);
- Simmons's as the first cookbook by an American printed in the United States.

**The *Smithsonian* article's byline** was not confirmed, so the source list
gives title, magazine and year only.

**Image substitutions** are recorded in `images/README.md`.
