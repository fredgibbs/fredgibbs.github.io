The board · 1 of 2 · what each one says history is for

Week 2 — the ancients

Herodotus · c. 430 BCE

Preserve renown

Memory and renown — that great deeds “may not lack renown” — and, almost in passing, the reason they went to war.

Thucydides · c. 400 BCE

See it coming

A warning you can reuse: a pattern in how power behaves, recognizable the next time it happens.

Sima Qian · c. 91 BCE

A complete record, and judged

“A universal net” over all the old traditions — sorted by type, and each chapter closed with a signed verdict.

Livy · from c. 27 BCE

So you have models

A “conspicuous monument” of examples: choose what to imitate, mark what to avoid, and save the state’s morals.

Week 3 — medieval and early modern

Bede · 731

To imitate the good

Livy’s preface with one clause added: the good is “worthy of the service of God.” History as the arrival of the faith.

A.-S. Chronicle · from c. 890

Provide basic catalog

No stated purpose at all. Kings, bishops, battles, portents — entered and left for the reader to make sense of.

al-Biruni · c. 1030

To ensure the record is not a lie

Truthfulness as a virtue in itself — and a duty to correct what you can and “leave the rest as it is.”

Machiavelli · 1513

To keep your state

Imitate “the paths beaten by great men” — not to become good, but to become effective.

The second question · what counts as proof

Three families, and they do not line up by date

01 · I was there, or I asked someone who was

Thucydides: only what he saw or had from reliable witnesses. Herodotus: travel, interviews, and a note where his sources disagree. The Chronicle: whatever the house happened to write down that year.

02 · It is in the tradition, and the tradition is the evidence

Livy: earlier writers, in a library — the legends he will “neither affirm nor refute.” Sima Qian: the imperial archives and every old story he can net. Bede: named informants, papal archives — and miracles, which are public, ongoing, and checkable.

03 · Interrogate the reporter — or skip the question

al-Biruni screens for physical possibility, then chases each claim back to its originator. Machiavelli barely asks: an example only has to demonstrate the mechanism. The most and least source-critical writers on this list are separated by five hundred years — in the wrong direction.

Where the disagreement isn’t

Three things all eight assume

01 · The past is for something

Not one of them writes to satisfy curiosity alone. Renown, warning, virtue, salvation, a judgment, a technique — every single one has a customer in mind. “Knowledge for its own sake” is not a premodern idea.

02 · Form carries the argument

A parable shape (Croesus, Lucretia), an invented speech (Pericles, the sparrow), a flat annual list, a table of contents that files a loser among sovereigns, a worked example with a body in the piazza. In every case the shape says something the facts don’t.

03 · Somebody decides what gets a line

Whole societies pass through these texts without a mention. Every one of these is an archive with a doorman — and the empty years in the Chronicle are just the most honest version of it.

Where the disagreement is

Three real fault lines

01 · Do you show the seams?

Yes: Herodotus names four incompatible stories; Livy will not affirm or refute the legends; al-Biruni publishes his rules first. No: Thucydides discloses once and then writes seamlessly; Bede’s sparrow speech carries no warning label. Confidence is not transparency.

02 · What kinds of things can happen?

The deepest split on the board, and it isn’t about rigor. Bede reasons carefully about a parted river. al-Biruni rules it inadmissible before asking who reported it. Both are scrupulous men. They disagree about the furniture of the world — and every evidence standard, ours included, rests on a claim like that.

03 · Is the lesson moral or technical?

Livy, Bede, and Sima Qian want you to become better. Machiavelli wants you to become effective. Same verb — imitate — and the histories they produce are unrecognizable to each other.

The trap · the story you will be tempted to tell

It doesn’t sort by date

“There have always been multiple perspectives from which the past can be viewed… the predominant ways of recording history have changed over time.” Popkin, p. 27 — against “a story of progress”

Try it and watch it fail

Rank the eight by how much they resemble what we do now. Thucydides (5th c. BCE) and al-Biruni (11th c. CE) land next to each other near the top. Livy (1st c. BCE) and Bede (8th c. CE) land next to each other well below. The order you get is nothing like chronological, which is exactly what a ladder cannot survive.

What Acton got wrong

He called the Middle Ages “a twilight of fiction” (p. 41) because he graded them on a purpose they never had. That is the same error as praising Thucydides for sounding rigorous: you are scoring resemblance to yourself and calling it progress.

Take home

Difference is the finding. If it were progress, the dates would cooperate — and they don’t.

Discussion · looking back across weeks 2 and 3

Which of these eight would recognize each other?

  • Line up their answers to what is history for. Where are the real disagreements, and where is it just different vocabulary for the same idea?
  • Thucydides wants only what he can verify; Bede treats miracles as proof; al-Biruni wants named sources and declared biases. Progress, or just difference?
  • Livy and Machiavelli both mine the Roman past for lessons — and Machiavelli read Livy closely. Do they want the same kind of lesson?
  • If you had to sit two of these eight at a table and have them agree they do the same job — which two, and what would they argue about first?

Next · week 4

Two more answers — each claiming to end the argument

4.1 · History has a direction

Voltaire and Kant: reason advances, civilization improves, and so the past is going somewhere. The first of our eight questions answered by a theory rather than a purpose.

4.2 · History can be a science

Ranke: show the past “as it actually was.” Not a new purpose — a claim to have no purpose at all, which is the most interesting position on the board.

The question to carry in

Both will present themselves as the end of the story you just spent two weeks refusing to tell. Ask of each: what is it for, and who is it for? The answers have never once been “nothing” and “no one.”
