Part one · Weeks 1, 3, 4, 6, 7

## Somebody chose the facts, often before any historian arrived
{: .main-point}
<div class="rule"></div>

Every history is a selection. Much of it was made before the historian started: in who kept records, what seemed worth a line, and who paid for the archive.
{: .detail .fragment}
</section>

<!-- ========== 4.3 RANKE'S FOOTNOTE ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Standards of evidence · 3 / 4 · Ranke, Week 4

## Ranke assumes the truth is in the papers of people who were there
{: .main-point}

<div class="with-figure wide" markdown="1">

<!-- ========== 4.4 VANSINA ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Standards of evidence · 4 / 4 · Vansina, Week 6
{: .eyebrow}

## Vansina drops the assumption that evidence has to be written
{: .main-point}

Islamic scholars had judged hadith by their chains of transmission for centuries (p. 30). Vansina keeps Ranke's rule, a link back to an observer.

</section>

## Progress was an idea with its own history
{: .main-point}

The take home

## You cannot measure progress until you say what history is for
{: .main-point}
<div class="rule"></div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack fragment compact" data-fragment-index="0" markdown="1">

###### On progress
{: .label}

Tests of sources were sharpened and spread (al-Biruni's screen, Ranke's footnote) and the range of evidence widened: oral tradition, the losers, gender. Carr's caution holds: "an infinity of meanings, none any more right than any other" amounts to no meaning at all (p. 26).
</div>

<div class="reveal-block argument fragment compact" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### On purpose
{: .label}

The choice of purpose shapes much of a history before the research begins. A defensible one can probably be stated openly, checked by someone who disagrees, and honest about its blind spots.
