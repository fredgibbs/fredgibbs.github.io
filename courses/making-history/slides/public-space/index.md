<!-- ========== WHAT MONUMENTS ARE FOR ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Loewen · "Some Functions of Public History"
{: .eyebrow}

## Are monuments for teaching the past?
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">
"Why have Americans put up hundreds of thousands of markers and monuments across the landscape?"
<div class="cite">Loewen, <i>Lies Across America</i>, p. 13</div>
</div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### His answer
{: .label}

Learning from the past is "hardly the only reason for public history," and "not even the primary reason" (p. 13). He starts with stories, class power and a civil religion (pp. 13–14).
</div>
</section>

## Human beings live by stories and monuments supply the heroes
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">

</div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

{: .label}

</div>
</section>

<!-- ========== CLASS ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Loewen · class
{: .eyebrow}

## Commemorating history helps the upper class stay upper
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">
"Usually, behind even the simplest aluminum plaque lurks someone who feels good about its installation. The efforts of the upper class to commemorate their history help them feel better psychologically; the results also help them stay upper. History is power."
<div class="cite">Loewen, <i>Lies Across America</i>, p. 13</div>
</div>

<div class="reveal-block argument compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### Who shapes the memory
{: .label}

Those on top of society know this, Loewen says, so "they take the time to determine how history will be remembered" (pp. 13–14).
</div>
</section>

<!-- ========== WHATEVER HAPPENED WAS GOOD ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Loewen · what monuments do to thinking
{: .eyebrow}

## Most monuments suggest that whatever happened was good
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="cards" markdown="1">
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### The state is rarely criticized

Most war memorials turn their conflicts into "noble causes." Markers rarely criticize the government (p. 14).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Thinking is discouraged

A modest plaque "speaks with authority," as if its message were "incontestable" (p. 14).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### The future inherits it

One generation's monuments become "a force imprisoning the minds" of the next. Local ones can "empower people" too (p. 17).
</div>
</div>
</section>

<!-- ========== WHO PUTS THEM UP ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Loewen · "The Sociology of Historic Sites"
{: .eyebrow}

## Monuments need approval, and only certain kinds go up
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="cards" markdown="1">
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### State marker offices

Early one-person offices made errors; review boards from the late 1950s did better. In Georgia, a legislator's call could still take a marker down (pp. 18–20).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Sponsors

Often a private club raises part of the cost and a city or state pays the rest. No relatively disinterested state body reviews monuments (pp. 20–21).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Who gets remembered

Indian wars, which many textbooks drop; labor, which has influence and money; and the upper class, "always" (pp. 21–22).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Happy history

Communities usually mark "only their happy history." Burlington honors Ethan Allen, whom Abenakis see as "a rapacious Indian-hater" (p. 23).
</div>
</div>
</section>

<!-- ========== HASSANAMESIT · NOVEMBER 1675 ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Hassanamesit · November 1675
{: .eyebrow}

## The marker's happy history simplifies the relationships
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">
"they will either force you all to some Island as the Natick Indians are, where you will be in danger to be starved with cold and hunger, and most probably in the end be all sent out of the country for slaves."
<div class="cite">Nipmuc fighters on the English, as reported by Daniel Gookin, 1677, p. 476</div>
</div>

<div class="reveal-block historical compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### Suspected by the English
{: .label}

Eleven Hassanamesit Christians were jailed in Boston for a murder, then acquitted (p. 476).
</div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="2" markdown="1">

###### Divided
{: .label}

About two hundred went with the fighters, some "with heavy hearts and weeping eyes." Job Kattenanit later went among them as a spy, seeking his children (pp. 476–77, 480).
</div>
</section>

Hassanamesit · James the Printer

## James the Printer joined Philip's side, then went back to the press
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="reveal-block historical compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### The marker's one named Nipmuc
{: .label}

Born at Hassanamesit, he enrolled at Harvard and was apprenticed to the Cambridge printer Samuel Green, whose press printed Eliot's Bible.
</div>

<div class="reveal-block argument compact fragment" data-fragment-index="2" markdown="1">

###### In the war
{: .label}

He served King Philip's allies as a scribe and diplomat. Afterward he returned to the Cambridge press, though some called him a traitor.
</div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="3" markdown="1">

###### Still in Grafton
{: .label}

The marker says Hassanamesit "was" his home. Hassanamisco Nipmuc families still hold a small reservation in Grafton.
</div>

Boston Athenaeum, "'We have but one man': A Rare Glimpse into the Life and Work of James (the) Printer."
{: .cite}
</section>

<!-- ========== PLACE ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Hayden · "place"
{: .eyebrow}

## Hayden joins the sense of place to the politics of space
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">
"'Place' is one of the trickiest words in the English language, a suitcase so overfilled one can never shut the lid. It carries the resonance of homestead, location, and open space in the city as well as a position in a social hierarchy."
<div class="cite">Hayden, <i>The Power of Place</i>, p. 15</div>
</div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### The sense of place
{: .label}

Place reaches all five senses, which makes it "powerful as a source of memory" (p. 18).
</div>

<div class="reveal-block argument compact fragment" data-fragment-index="2" markdown="1">

###### The politics of space
{: .label}

Phrases like "knowing one's place" are spatial and political at once (p. 16). Cultural geography, she says, kept to rural landscapes, "avoiding issues of political contestation" (p. 17).
</div>
</section>

<!-- ========== THREE HISTORIES ========== -->
<section markdown="1">
Hayden · three histories to write
{: .eyebrow}

## Three kinds of history a historian can write
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="cards" markdown="1">
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Working landscapes

Who built and kept up the farms, mines, railroads and streetcars, a history that has "barely been studied" (pp. 20–22).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Territorial histories

Ghettos and barrios, reservations and camps; for women, the body, the home and the street: territories with enforced boundaries (pp. 22–29).
</div>
<div class="card" markdown="1">

#### Building types

The political life of ordinary buildings: who built, owned, regulated and rented them (pp. 30–34).
</div>
</div>
</section>

## Keeping people out of space is a way of limiting rights
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">
"One of the consistent ways to limit the economic and political rights of groups has been to constrain social reproduction by limiting access to space."

</div>

## Hayden reads an ordinary building as a record of power
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="quote" markdown="1">

Wilson · *The Myth of Santa Fe*, 1997
{: .eyebrow}

## Wilson argues that Santa Fe's famous look was built in the early twentieth century
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="reveal-block historical compact" markdown="1">

###### Chris Wilson
{: .label}

Founding director of UNM's Historic Preservation and Regionalism program. His book asks how a small capital of about 5,000 people in 1912 got the look it is known for.
</div>

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### How it was made
{: .label}

Largely by Anglo-American newcomers, Wilson argues. After statehood in 1912, leaders built the town's economy on its heritage: restorations that were partly speculation, visible signs of Americanization removed, new buildings put under design review.
</div>

<div class="reveal-block argument compact fragment" data-fragment-index="2" markdown="1">

###### The story it tells
{: .label}

Three peoples living in harmony. Wilson argues that story oversimplifies Native American culture, the mixing of cultures, and the tensions between them.
</div>
</section>

<!-- ========== THE TAKE HOME ========== -->
<section data-transition="fade" markdown="1">
The take home
{: .eyebrow}

## Hayden argues that places record who made them and who was kept out
{: .main-point}

---

<div class="reveal-block unpack compact fragment" data-fragment-index="1" markdown="1">

###### Places are produced
{: .label}

Ditchdiggers and housewives, as well as architects and mayors, shaped the city (p. 15). A tunnel, a movie house or a tenement records who built it, who used it and who was kept out (pp. 20–34).
</div>

<div class="takehome fragment compact" data-fragment-index="2" markdown="1">
Limiting access to space is a consistent way to limit a group's economic and political rights (p. 22). A building can record who was let in.
</div>
</section>
