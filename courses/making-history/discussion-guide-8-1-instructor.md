# 8.1 Discussion Guide — Instructor Version

**Not a student page.** An outline of the big ideas that run across Weeks 1–7, each with the historical detail that supports it and a question or two to put to the room. They feed the two discussion questions on the [schedule](schedule) for 8.1, which frame the [Historians Café](historians-cafe) due tonight:

1. **Is there any "progress" in historical writing?** Ranke corrects Voltaire, Marx corrects Ranke, Thompson corrects Marx, Ginzburg corrects Thompson. Closer to the truth, or more frameworks?
2. **What is history for?** Livy, moral examples; Voltaire, civilizational lessons; Ranke, truth; Marx, revolution; Thompson, rescuing the forgotten. Which is most defensible?

Quotations are the ones already checked in the lecture decks, with their page cites.

---

## 1. Somebody chose the facts before any historian arrived

*Feeds both questions.*

**The idea.** Every history is a selection, and much of the selecting happened before the historian started: in who kept records, what they thought worth a line, and who paid for the archive.

**Historical detail**
- **Carr, Week 1.** "The facts speak only when the historian calls on them" (*What Is History?*, p. 11). Caesar crossing the Rubicon is a historical fact; millions of other people crossing the same "petty stream" interest "nobody at all." Our picture of fifth-century Greece was formed by "a tiny group of people in the city of Athens" (pp. 13–14).
- **The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, Week 3.** Some years are only a number: "A. 786." The 793 sack of Lindisfarne, conventionally the start of the Viking age, gets one clause, about as much as one man's death in the same entry.
- **Who could write.** After Rome fell in the west, the clergy "would provide almost all of the authors of history for the next several centuries" (Popkin, p. 41), and monastic chronicles "often reflected the influence of local rulers who protected the institution" (p. 43).
- **The state behind the archive, Week 4.** The University of Berlin was founded in 1810 "as part of a deliberate program to promote German national identity" (Popkin, p. 79); Prussia launched the *Monumenta Germaniae Historica* in 1819 to strengthen national consciousness (p. 84). Women "remained almost entirely excluded" from the seminars that made professional historians (p. 94).
- **Whose words survive, Weeks 6–7.** Thompson's working people come through spy reports and trials. Ginzburg's miller can be heard only because the Inquisition wrote down his answers.

**To ask the room**
- If the record was filtered before anyone wrote history from it, can a better method undo the filter, or only work around it?
- What did it never occur to these record-keepers to write down?

## 2. Every writer tells you what history is for, and the purpose shapes the history

*Question 2 directly.*

**The idea.** Nearly every writer in the course states, or implies, a job for the past; Ranke's is to refuse the others. The job decides what counts as worth telling.

**Historical detail**
- **Herodotus:** memory and renown, so "that the memory of the past may not be blotted out" (*Histories* 1.1).
- **Thucydides:** read ever since as "a warning against the dangers that can befall a society that overestimates its own strength" (Popkin, p. 29).
- **Livy:** a stock of examples; "choose for yourself and for your own state what to imitate" (Preface). **Bede** repeats the job seven and a half centuries later (imitate the good, shun the wrong) and adds only that the good is worthy of the service of God.
- **Sima Qian:** a complete record, "a universal net to gather together all the old traditions of the world" (quoted in Popkin, p. 37), with his own signed verdict closing 106 of the 130 chapters.
- **Machiavelli:** the same verb, *imitate*, with a different payoff. He wrote a whole book of commentaries on Livy and drew technique, not virtue, from the same Rome.
- **Voltaire, Kant, Ranke, Marx, Thompson:** taste and manners; a plan of Nature; "only" what actually happened; a motor heading to revolution; a rescue "from the enormous condescension of posterity" (Thompson, p. 12).
- **Same source, two histories.** An inquisition trial record is a church tribunal's record. Read as Ranke read his sources, for what officials did and decided, it gives a procedure; read by Ginzburg for what the miller believed, it gives a cosmos.

**To ask the room**
- Ranke says he refuses the jobs of "judging the past and of instructing the present." Is refusing a purpose itself a purpose?
- Which two of these purposes could one person hold at once, and where would they pull against each other?

## 3. The shape of the story is part of the argument

*Feeds question 1: form changes, but it is never neutral.*

**The idea.** Before method, each writer chose a form, and the form does argumentative work the facts can't do on their own.

**Historical detail**
- **Herodotus's Croesus** arrives with a parable's shape: a warning ignored, an ambiguous oracle, pride, reversal, recognition on the pyre. Popkin notes he has also been called "the father of lies" (pp. 30–31).
- **Thucydides** composes the speeches himself, making speakers "say what, in my opinion, was called for by each situation" (1.22), and stages Melos as a dialogue: "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" (5.89, Crawley trans.; the assigned PDF reads "the weak submit").
- **Livy** turns the end of the Roman monarchy on one household (Lucretia), and reuses a story Herodotus tells too: a tyrant answering a messenger by striking off the tallest plants (Herodotus 5.92; Livy 1.54).
- **Sima Qian** files Xiang Yu, who lost and never ruled a unified empire, among the basic annals of legitimate sovereigns: an argument made by the table of contents.
- **Annals** began as notes in the margins of Easter tables, a line per year whether or not anything happened. "No interpretation" is still a choice about what deserves a line.
- **Braudel, Week 5:** "narrative history is not an objective method, still less the supreme objective method, but is itself a philosophy of history" (*On History*, p. 4).

**To ask the room**
- Is a footnoted, neutral-sounding history a form too? What does it argue by its shape?

## 4. Standards of evidence keep changing, and each rests on a belief about the world

*Question 1 directly: this is where real gains can be named, and where they get complicated.*

**The idea.** Every period in the course had rules for proof. The rules differ less in rigour than in their assumptions about what can happen.

**Historical detail**
- **Bede** treats miracles as evidence and reasons about them carefully. Alban's river dries up; the cures at his shrine "cease not to this day," an appeal to evidence anyone could go and check.
- **Al-Biruni** sets out rules before he begins. "Hearsay does not equal eyewitness"; he names five kinds of liar and chases a report back until "there remains the originator of the story." But a report that breaks natural law is ruled out *before* he asks who told it. Bede's river would never reach the interview.
- **Valla, 1440,** shows the Donation of Constantine is a forgery from its Latin, which uses words that did not exist when it was supposedly written: language carries its own date. He wrote it for a king at war with the pope; the result holds anyway. It sat unprinted until 1517.
- **Ranke, 1824:** a hierarchy of sources, "noted on every page," and a seminar where students attacked one another's citations. The method catches errors; it cannot catch an assumption the whole room shares.
- **The *Annales*, Week 5:** price series, harvest records, field patterns: evidence that is nobody's testimony. Bloch: the strips of northern French fields owe more to "the settlers in the era of the dolmens" than to "the lawyers of the First Empire" (*The Historian's Craft*, p. 40).
- **Vansina, Week 6:** oral tradition as "a series of successive historical documents all lost except for the last one" (*Oral Tradition as History*, p. 29). He notes Islamic scholars were judging chains of transmission for hadith since the second century after the Hijra (p. 30).

**To ask the room**
- Al-Biruni had the eyewitness test some eight centuries before Ranke. If the method was already there, what exactly progressed?
- Which of our own evidence rules rest on a belief about the world we never state, the way Bede's and al-Biruni's did?

## 5. What moves history, and at what scale

*Both questions: the course's running question, "what makes history happen?"*

**The idea.** The course's writers disagree about what causes change, and each answer comes with a scale that decides what it can see.

**Historical detail**
- **God:** Bede reads English history as Christianity's arrival. **Machiavelli** keeps God in the story but explains by technique: Moses goes in a list with Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus, analysed by the same criteria.
- **Nature's plan:** Kant's history runs on "unsocial sociability" and vanity, not virtue, and he admits that "from such crooked wood as man is made of, nothing perfectly straight can be built" (Sixth Thesis).
- **Decisions:** Ranke's sources are ambassadors' reports and memoirs: what participants saw and chose.
- **Structures:** Marx's motor is production and class: "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." After 1848 he adds that people make their own history "but they do not make it just as they please" (*Eighteenth Brumaire*, in Green and Troup, p. 36). Braudel's structures are prisons; events are "a surface disturbance." Lepanto killed tens of thousands in an afternoon, yet the Ottomans rebuilt their fleet and Venice made terms by 1573.
- **People:** Thompson's working class "was present at its own making" (p. 9). **Scott** adds an axis rather than a scale: "high politics itself is a gendered concept," built on excluding women (p. 1073).
- **One case:** Ginzburg's single miller gives a mind in full and cannot say whether it was typical.
- **The trade-off:** zoom out and you can explain everything and decide nothing; zoom in and you can decide everything and explain almost nothing.

**To ask the room**
- Does the choice of scale follow from what a historian thinks history is for, or the other way round?

## 6. "Progress" was an idea with its own history

*Question 1 directly: the course's own subject matter cuts against reading it as a ladder.*

**The idea.** The belief that history improves was argued for in the eighteenth century, contested at once, and refused by the founder of professional history. The course's own order invites the progress reading, and Popkin warns against it.

**Historical detail**
- **Voltaire** finds only four "happy ages," picked by "whosoever has taste," and has all Europe indebted to "the court of Louis XIV" (*Age of Louis XIV*, pp. 5, 7–8).
- **Condorcet,** writing in hiding during the Revolution, holds that "nature has set no term to the perfection of human faculties" (quoted in Popkin, p. 67). **Ferguson** turns progress into stages that any society can be placed on.
- **Herder,** Kant's own former student, objects to measuring nations against "a universal standard of civilization derived from the experience of Western Europe" (Popkin's paraphrase, p. 68).
- **Ranke, 1854,** refuses the ladder: "every epoch is immediate to God," and treating earlier generations as stepping stones is "a divine injustice." The same lecture still calls Asia's history "retrogressive."
- **Lord Acton, 1895,** dismisses medieval historians as "content to be deceived, to live in a twilight of fiction" (quoted in Popkin, pp. 40–41): the progress story told with full confidence.
- **Popkin, Week 2:** for much of the twentieth century "the history of history was written as a story of progress," whereas "there have always been multiple perspectives from which the past can be viewed" (pp. 26–27).

**To ask the room**
- Each turn on the syllabus fixed something. What did each one give up?
- How will a historian in 2150 describe us, the way Acton described the chroniclers?

## Pulling it together

Two landing points the outline supports, offered as positions to argue with rather than answers:

- **On progress:** narrow, checkable things did improve. Tests of sources were sharpened and spread (Valla's anachronism, Ranke's footnote; the chain of transmission is older than either), and the range of evidence widened (oral tradition, the record of the losers, gender). The big question stays open because there was never one agreed target. As Carr warns, that is not the same as saying every account is as good as any other: "an infinity of meanings, none any more right than any other … comes to much the same thing" as no meaning at all (p. 26).
- **On purpose:** the choice of purpose decides much of the history before a source is read. A defensible purpose is probably one that can be stated openly, that someone else can check the work against, and whose blind spots can be named; most of the writers in the course meet one or two of those, not all three.
