# Why Study History?

Most people who say they hate history don't actually hate history. They hate having to memorize what seems to be pointless infomation and regurgitate it, and where the answer to "why does this matter?" was either "it's on the test" or "those who don't learn from the past are condemmed to repeat it" — which makes no sense since we all know history repeats itself. Luckily, studying history has a lot more to offer.

## Every "why" question has two _kinds_ of answers
Ask why rent near campus is so high and you'll get an immediate answer: vacancy rates, interest rates, how many people want to live here. Correct. But there's a second kind of answer — which neighborhoods got built and which got bulldozed, who was allowed to buy property here decades ago, who had access to capital, how UNM dorms have affected the housing market, what the city decided to spend money on sixty years ago. The first answer explains this month. The second explains why this decade looks the way it does at all.

Neither one is the "real" answer. They're two different questions that live at different scales. What's useful is being able to move between them — knowing when the immediate explanation is enough and when it's covering up the more interesting problem. People who can only give the first kind of explanation spend a lot of time solving the wrong problems.

That's what history is: the ability to understand why at different scales in different contexts. 


## Nothing around you is natural
The forty-hour work week. Where the border is. What counts as a good job. Who is allowed to marry whom. Why the school year has a summer in it. Which part of town got the highway and which got the park. Some of these were contested; some were decided; some just happened by circumstance. Arrangements that stop being visible start to feel natural, and things that feel natural are very hard to argue with.

History is largely the study of how things that feel inevitable got that way. Once you can see the decision underneath the arrangement, the arrangement stops being fate and becomes a choice — one that somebody made, for reasons, and could have made differently.


## Why people do things that make no sense
People in the past believed in witches, went to war over insults, tolerated arrangements we'd find unbearable, and passed up opportunities that look obvious from here. The easy response is that they were ignorant, or that people were just different then. 

The historical response, however, is to assume they had reasons, and then go find out what the reasons were — what the world looked like from inside their situation, what they were afraid of, what options they thought they had.

That is hopefully one of the key points this class leaves you with. Someone holds a position you find incomprehensible — about religion, or politics, or how to raise kids, or what happened in this country in 2020. You can decide they're stupid, which explains nothing and predicts nothing. Or you can ask what has to be true about their situation for that position to make sense to them. There's also a harder version of this point. History is the only evidence we have about human beings at scale. A chemist can run the reaction again; nobody can rerun the Great Depression under slightly different conditions. So every claim about how people behave in groups — that inflation topples governments, that immigrants assimilate, that protest works, that empires overreach, that new technology is making us stupid — is a claim about what has happened before. There is no other data set. When a politician or an economist or your uncle tells you what people are like, they're making a historical argument whether they know it or not, and knowing how to recognize that is a life skill.

## Who you think you are is a history
Identity runs on stories about the past. Your family's account of how it ended up here, and what it survived to do it. What your hometown thinks it is. What a religion remembers about its founding. What "American" is supposed to mean, and who that word has included at various points. These stories tell people who their people are, what they're owed, and what they owe — which is an enormous amount of work for a story to do.

They're also selective, like all histories. Families keep some ancestors and quietly drop others. Nations remember their founding and forget who was already there. None of that makes the stories worthless; it makes them made. Knowing they were made, and being able to ask what got left out, changes your relationship to the ones you inherited without requiring you to throw them away.

This is also why arguments about history get so much angrier than the stakes seem to justify. A fight about a monument, or a textbook, or a date on a plaque, is rarely about the fact in question. It's about who a group is now. When people say "that's not who we are," they are making a historical claim — and usually a contested one.


## History is everywhere
Monuments, and the fights about taking them down. The version of the founding you got in fourth grade. A company's origin story. A country's account of a war it lost. A politician explaining what this nation has always believed. Advertising that's nostalgic for a decade nobody actually lived through. Anyone who says "we've always done it this way."

All of these are histories: selective accounts of the past, assembled in the present, for a purpose. They aren't necessarily lies. But somebody built them, for something, and left things out in order to do it. Being able to notice that — to see the seams, to ask what got trimmed and who benefits from the trimming — isn't cynicism. It's just literacy. And it happens to be the actual subject of this course.


## Ways of thinking you take with you
Four habits, and none of them are really about the past. Apply them on whatever is in front of you.

**Looking for the structure under the situation.** The immediate explanation is almost never the whole explanation. Asking what arrangement produced this situation, and who set it up, is a move you can make about a policy, a workplace, a family, or a city block.

**Explaining people instead of judging them.** Assume there's a reason, then go find it. This is the difference between understanding a group and having an opinion about it.

**Asking how anyone knows.** Every account came from somewhere. Where? Who wrote it down, who didn't get the chance, and what survived by accident?

**Living with disagreement.** Two careful people can read the same evidence and reach different conclusions. That doesn't mean one of them is lying, and it doesn't mean it's all just opinion. Getting comfortable in that space — judging which account is better supported without needing there to be one right answer — is useful well beyond this class.


## Back to the objection
The reason to study history isn't that the past is interesting, although it is. It's that the present is confusing — the people in it, the arrangements you were born into, the stories you were handed about who you are — and history is the only tool we have for working out how any of it got this way, and making informed decisions about where it should go. 

That's what the rest of the semester is about.
