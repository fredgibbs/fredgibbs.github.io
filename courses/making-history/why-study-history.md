# Why Study History?

Most people who say they hate history don't actually hate history. They hate having to memorize what looks like pointless information and hand it back on a test, where the answer to "why does this matter?" was either "it's on the test" or "those who don't learn from the past are condemned to repeat it" — which is less an argument than a vague threat. Nobody ever says which part we're about to repeat, or what learning from it would have looked like.

History isn't useful because the past is useful. Most of it isn't, at least not directly. History is useful because working out *why* something happened and *why* it mattered provides practice thinking across scales. What you take away at the end isn't the past. It's a technique for figuring out why anything is the way it is.

## Every "why" question has two _kinds_ of answers
Ask why rent near campus is so high and you'll get an immediate answer: vacancy rates, interest rates, how many people want to live here. Correct. But there's a second kind of answer — which neighborhoods got built and which got bulldozed, who was allowed to buy property here decades ago, who had access to capital, how UNM dorms have affected the housing market, what the city decided to spend money on forty years ago. The first answer explains this month. The second explains why this decade.

Neither one is the "real" answer. They're different questions living at different scales. Ask why the bus is late and the answer is traffic, not post WWII suburban sprawl or the history of municipal transit funding — sometimes the person asking just wanted the "now" answer. But people who can only ever give the first kind of explanation spend a lot of time solving the wrong problem.


## Countries don't do things
France invaded. The Church opposed it. The company decided to relocate. The seventies turned against public housing.

Every one of those sentences is shorthand, and every one hides the thing you probably wanted to know. France didn't invade — a few men in a room, with particular interests, particular fears, and certain information, decided to invade, and then several hundred thousand people who had never met them carried it out for reasons entirely their own: duty, pay, conscription, belief, boredom, nowhere else to go. "The Church opposed it" covers bishops with careers, religious orders with rivalries, and parish priests who ignored the whole business.

But you can't work down at that scale all the time either. Sometimes "France invaded" is exactly the right level of description, because what you're explaining is a high-level pattern that individual intentions don't really touch — a trade route, a border, a century of tariffs. 

So there are at least three scales in play for almost any historical question: the structural conditions, the institutions and the people running them, and the individuals living on the ground. A good explanation tells you which one it's working at. A bad one slides between them without noticing — which is how you get "Rome fell because Romans got decadent," a sentence that sounds like a cause and is actually just a mood.

This is the skill. Not knowing what happened to Rome. Being able to hear a claim about why *anything* happened — an election, a layoff, a war, a breakup — and ask what scale it's pitched at, what it's compressing, and whether that compression is doing honest work or quietly hiding the argument.

## Nothing around you is natural
The forty-hour work week. Where the border is. What counts as a good job. Who is allowed to marry whom. Why the school year has a summer in it. Which part of town got the highway and which got the park. Some of these were contested; some were decided; some just happened by circumstance. Arrangements that stop being visible start to feel natural, and things that feel natural are very hard to argue with.

History is largely the study of how things that feel inevitable got that way. Once you can see the decision underneath the arrangement, the arrangement stops being fate and becomes a choice — one that somebody made, for reasons, and could have made differently.

This is what historians mean when they say the discipline is the study of change over time. They don't mean timelines. They mean that everything around you is mid-motion: it was different before, it will be different later, and the real question is what's driving the change and what's holding it still.

## People in the past had reasons
People in the past believed in witches, went to war over insults, tolerated arrangements we'd find unbearable, and passed up opportunities that look obvious from here. The easy response is that they were ignorant, or that people were just different then.

The historical response is to assume they had reasons, and then go find out what the reasons were — what the world looked like from inside their situation, what they were afraid of, what options they thought they had.

This is also the hardest thing history does, and it's worth knowing up front that it often fails. Why someone did something is almost never written down, and when it is, people lie, posture, and misunderstand themselves as readily as anyone you know. We can usually establish basic historical "facts", like who, what, when, and where. Why has to be inferred — argued from evidence, held with some humility, and revised when better evidence turns up. The thing we most want to know is the thing we can least confirm.

That habit is one of the key things I hope this class leaves you with. Someone holds a position you find incomprehensible — about religion, or politics, or how to raise kids, or what happened in this country in 2020. You can decide they're stupid, which explains nothing and predicts nothing. Or you can ask what would have to be true about their situation for that position to make sense to them. Understanding why someone believes something is not the same as agreeing with them, and it doesn't replace judging whether you think they're right. It's what makes the judgment worth anything.

## The only data we have on people
There's a harder reason this matters. History is the only evidence we have about human beings at scale. A chemist can run the reaction again; nobody can rerun the Great Depression under slightly different conditions. So every claim about how people behave in groups — that inflation topples governments, that immigrants assimilate, that protest works, that empires overreach, that new technology is making us stupid — is a claim about what has happened before. There is no other data set.

When a politician or an economist or your uncle tells you what people are like, they're making a historical argument. Being being able to recognize assumptions built into simple statements is a key civic duty. It's also, unglamorously, what a functioning electorate runs on. Nearly every policy question you will ever vote on contains a buried claim about how people have behaved before, how the other side has it all wrong, usually offered by someone who is hoping you won't think for yourself.

## Who you think you are is a history
Identity runs on stories about the past. Your family's account of how it ended up here, and what it survived to do it. What your hometown thinks it is. What a religion remembers about its founding. What "American" is supposed to mean, and who that word has included at various points. These stories tell people who their people are, what they're owed, and what they owe — which is an enormous amount of work for a story to do.

They're also selective, like all histories. Families keep some ancestors and quietly drop others. Nations remember their founding and forget who was already there. None of that makes the stories worthless; it makes them *made*. Knowing they were made, and being able to ask what got left out, changes your relationship to the ones you inherited without requiring you to throw them away.

This is also why arguments about history get so much angrier than the stakes seem to justify. A fight about a monument, or a textbook, or a date on a plaque, is rarely about the fact in question. It's about the power to shape stories, pasts, and futures. 

## We make history
Monuments, and the fights about taking them down. The version of the founding you got in fourth grade. A company's origin story. A country's account of a war it lost. A politician explaining what this nation has always believed. Advertising nostalgic for a decade nobody actually lived through. Anyone who says "we've always done it this way." An AI summarizing a topic for you in four confident paragraphs, assembled from sources it won't name.

All of these are histories: selective accounts of the past, assembled in the present, for a purpose. They aren't necessarily lies. But somebody built them, for something, and left things out in order to do it. Being able to notice that — to see the seams, to ask what got trimmed and who benefits from the trimming — isn't cynicism. It's literacy.

The historian Alice Dreger puts it this way: although history makes us, we make history. Nobody has a time machine. Everything we think we know about the past is a story somebody constructed out of whatever happened to survive. That's the pun in this course's title, and it's the actual subject — not what happened, but how societies have gone about making accounts of what happened, and why the rules for doing it keep changing. What counted as good evidence for an ancient historian isn't the same for a medieval chronicler, or for a nineteenth-century German professor trying to turn history into a science, or for whatever generated your search results this morning. Truth, evidence, and authority have histories of their own. Tracing those is what we're spending the semester on.

## Ways of thinking you take with you
Four habits, and none of them are really about the past. Three are the kinds of thinking listed early on the [syllabus](index).

**Structural analysis.** The immediate explanation is almost never the whole explanation. Asking what arrangement produced this situation, who set it up, and what scale it operates at is a move you can make about a policy, a workplace, a family, or a city block.

**Source criticism.** Every account came from somewhere. Who made it, for whom, in what context, and what did it leave out? Who wrote things down, who never got the chance, and what survived by accident? This works identically on a medieval chronicle, a news article, your uncle's Facebook post, and an AI-generated summary.

**Argument analysis.** Separate what someone is claiming from the evidence they've actually offered, then ask whether the second supports the first. Most bad arguments aren't false exactly — they're underbuilt, and they get by on confidence.

Running underneath all three is a tolerance you have to build: two careful people can read the same evidence and reach different conclusions. That doesn't mean one of them is lying, and it doesn't mean it's all just opinion. Getting comfortable in that space — judging which account is better supported without needing there to be one right answer — is most of what intellectual maturity consists of, and it's the single most portable thing in this course. Out in the world people call it analysis. This is it.

## Back to the objection
The reason to study history isn't that the past is interesting, although it is. It's that the present is confusing — the people in it, the arrangements you were born into, the stories you were handed about who you are — and every explanation anyone offers you for any of it is a causal claim, pitched at some scale, built from some evidence, by somebody with a purpose.

So the rest of the semester is about the history of history, but the history is a means to an end. The end is twofold: 1) learning how people are always repackaging stories as "history" and "truth" for particular puposes. 2) Understanding that unless you're doing a math problem set, there's never a right answer, just interpretation, argument, and evidence.
