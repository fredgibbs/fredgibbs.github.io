# Week 2 Discussion Notes — Instructor

**Not a student page.** Notes for running the two discussion slides in the [Evidence, Purpose, and Form](slides/greeks-evidence-and-purpose/) deck. For each question: what you're actually fishing for, the passage to point at, the answer students give first, and the follow-up that gets past it.

---

## 2.1 — Let's argue about it

Budget roughly 20 minutes for Q1–Q3, which are the load-bearing ones. Q4 and Q5 are there so you have somewhere to go; the two-sentence exercise is the fallback if the room won't talk at all.

### Q1. What counts as *evidence* for Herodotus? For Thucydides? Point at a passage.

**After:** Getting them to notice that both men have a procedure, and that the procedures differ in *what they do with disagreement* — not in whether they care about truth.

**Point them at:** Herodotus 1.1–5 (the Persian and Phoenician accounts of who started it, then "I will not say that these things happened thus or otherwise") against Thucydides 1.22 (deeds tested "as far as possible" against multiple witnesses; speeches composed as *ta deonta*, what the situation demanded).

**First answers you'll get:** "Herodotus uses hearsay, Thucydides uses facts." This is the cliché the deck spent two slides dismantling, and it will come back anyway. Don't crush it — make them test it.

**The follow-up:** *If Thucydides only reports what he verified, where did he get the Melian Dialogue?* Nobody friendly to Athens survived Melos, and he was in exile. The methods statement and the most famous scene in the book are in open tension, and Thucydides tells you this himself at 1.22.

**Land it on:** Herodotus shows his seams; Thucydides sands them off. That's a difference in *presentation* at least as much as in evidence.

### Q2. Herodotus tells you when his sources disagree. Thucydides tells you he made the speakers say what was *called for*. Which one is hiding more from you?

**After:** The realization that transparency about method and confidence in the result trade off against each other — and that we've inherited a prejudice for the confident one.

**The move:** Ask which one a modern historian would get in trouble for. They'll say Herodotus. Then ask which one a modern *journalist* would get fired for. The answer flips. Same practice, different professional norms — which is the whole point of the course.

**Watch for:** Someone will defend Thucydides on grounds that the speeches capture "what was essentially said." Good — push on *essentially*. Who decides what's essential? Thucydides does, silently, and the reader can't check him.

**Land it on:** Herodotus's disclosure is a cost he pays in authority. Thucydides's silence is authority he buys on credit.

### Q3. Why give Melos a *dialogue*? What does the form let him do that plain narration wouldn't?

**After:** Form as argument — the week's thesis, and the thing the refractions should be reaching for.

**Point them at:** 5.84–116, and note it's a formal departure. Thucydides narrates in his own voice everywhere else. Here he steps out entirely.

**What the form buys him:**

- Nobody is *reporting* the Athenian position, so nobody is responsible for it. It arrives unmediated.
- Dialogue strips the moral vocabulary. Once the Melians appeal to justice and the Athenians simply decline the category ("the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must," 5.89), the argument is over without Thucydides having to say so.
- It reads as a demonstration rather than a judgment, so it feels like watching rather than being told.

**The hard follow-up:** *Is this the most or the least honest thing in the book?* Both defenses are real. He's showing you power's logic with nothing on top — and he's putting words in dead men's mouths and letting the form do his condemning for him.

### Q4. Popkin compares the invented speeches to dialogue in historical films. Fair, or unfair?

**After:** A live test case for "different norms, not worse history."

**The unfair side:** A film knows it's a film; the audience has consented to invention. Thucydides claims a method three paragraphs earlier.

**The fair side:** Both are reconstructing what *must have been said* from what's known. Both are judged by plausibility. Neither audience expects a transcript — and Greek readers didn't either, which is the part students miss.

**Land it on:** The analogy holds for the technique and breaks on the contract with the reader. That distinction — technique vs. contract — is worth naming out loud; it comes back with the 1619 Project in week 12.

### Q5. Is Herodotus a historian or a storyteller — and does the distinction survive contact with either text?

Use this to close. It should now feel like a bad question, and they should be able to say why. If someone says "it's a false binary," make them cash it out with a passage: Thucydides shaping Melos for effect, or Herodotus naming sources and weighing them at 1.5.

**Land the day on:** Neither man is a rough draft of us. Each solved a problem we still have, and chose a different thing to sacrifice.

### If there's time — the two-sentence exercise

Three minutes writing, then take four or five aloud. Don't discuss them at length; the point is kinesthetic.

**What to listen for:** The Herodotus sentences are almost always longer and hedge with attribution ("according to X, though others say"). The Thucydides sentences are shorter and land harder. Then ask which they'd rather have been handed — most say Thucydides — and follow with *which one leaves you able to check the writer?* That reversal is the takeaway.

## 2.2 — Where they agree, and where they don't

The parallel block on the slide (lesson *inside* the story vs. lesson *appended*) is the frame. Get it on the board before the questions.

### Q1. Livy's preface says history supplies moral examples. Does the Lucretia passage actually do that? How?

**After:** Making them locate the mechanism, not just assert that it's moralizing.

**Point them at:** the preface (*Praef.* 10, the monument passage — *behold*, *choose*, *mark for avoidance*) against 1.58, where Lucretia says she acquits herself of the sin but not the penalty, so that "no unchaste woman shall henceforth live and plead Lucretia's example."

**The thing to surface:** She states the *exemplum* herself. Livy doesn't have to moralize because his character does it for him — and she moralizes in the vocabulary of legal precedent. The preface promised a stock of examples to draw on; the scene manufactures one on the spot and tells you how to file it.

**Good complication if someone's sharp:** Whose example is it? Livy's preface addresses male readers choosing models for themselves and the state. Lucretia's line is addressed to women. The scene is doing two jobs at once, and the Republic-founding one is Brutus's, not hers.

### Q2. Both use a political collapse to argue about power and virtue. Does the argument survive if the details were invented?

**After:** The distinction between a claim's truth and its warrant — and their discomfort at how well invented material can work.

**Run it as a split.** Take a show of hands first, then argue.

- *Survives:* the claim is about how tyranny and virtue operate, and a well-made example demonstrates that regardless of whether it happened. This is roughly how parables and case law work.
- *Doesn't survive:* Livy is telling you where your government came from. If it didn't happen, the founding charter is fiction, and the moral authority evaporates with the facts.

**Push with:** *Does your answer change depending on whether the reader knows it's invented?* Livy's own readers largely did — he tells them at *Praef.* 6 that he'll neither affirm nor refute the pre-foundational material. That's a genuinely different contract from the one modern readers assume.

### Q3. Which is more honest about what a historian does — Livy's silence, or Sima Qian's signed verdict?

**After:** Their instinct that "objective" means "invisible," and a chance to break it.

**Most will say Sima Qian**, because the judgment is labeled and therefore refusable. Let that build, then complicate it:

- The signed verdict comes *after* he's given Xiang Yu the song, the horse, the ferryman, the head. The sympathy is already installed. The box at the end looks like restraint but arrives on top of a highly worked narrative.
- And ask what Livy's silence actually is. He isn't hiding a judgment — the judgment is the shape of the story. Is a hidden verdict dishonest, or just a different location for the same thing?

**Land it on:** Every one of the four has a verdict. Herodotus won't referee, Thucydides referees invisibly, Livy lets the story referee, Sima Qian referees in a labeled box. The question is never *whether* the historian judges but *where they put it* — and how easily you can find and argue with it.

### Q4. Why do unconnected cultures all decide the past should be *useful*? A fact about history, or about the states that pay historians?

**After:** The first structural argument of the semester — the one that pays off in weeks 5 and 9.

**Set the independence up properly:** Sima Qian finishes around sixty years before Livy starts. No contact, no shared tradition, and both land on utility. That's the puzzle.

**The two answers, and don't resolve it:**

- *About history:* narrative about the past is how humans reason about what to do next; nobody funds an account of the past that yields nothing.
- *About patronage:* Sima Qian is a court official of Emperor Wu. Livy writes as Augustus promises moral restoration. Both are producing usable pasts for regimes that want one. "Useful" may just be what survives when the state is the audience.

**The turn that makes it land:** Ask who pays for history now — universities, museums, streaming services, state legislatures — and what kind of usefulness each wants. Then note that we're standing inside the same structure and calling it neutral. That's the water we're swimming in, back to week 1.

## If the room won't talk

- **Make it a vote first.** Every question above works as a hands-up split before it works as a discussion. People argue more readily once they've committed.
- **Make them point at a passage.** "Which sentence?" converts opinion into reading, and it's the move you want them practicing anyway.
- **Use the parallel on the board.** Four columns — Herodotus, Thucydides, Livy, Sima Qian — and one row: *where does the judgment live?* Fill it together. Nearly every question above is a way into that row.
- **Fall back to the two-sentence exercise.** It's on the 2.1 slide but works on either day.

## The board diagram, if you want one

|  | Herodotus | Thucydides | Livy | Sima Qian |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Where the judgment lives | Declines to make one | Inside the speeches | Inside the story's shape | In a signed box at the end |
| What you can check | His sources, because he names them | The deeds, not the speeches | Nothing | The verdict, not the narrative |
| What history is for | Memory and renown | A reusable warning | A stock of *exempla* | A record with a ruling attached |

The bottom row is the week's take-home. The middle row is the one that surprises them.
