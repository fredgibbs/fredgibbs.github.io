# Advanced Lecture Readings — Instructor Version

**Not a student page.** This collects the 📙 readings from the [schedule](schedule) in one place, with what each is doing in lecture and which student reading it sits behind. The student-facing description of 📙 is "advanced reading and lecture reference — no need to read unless part of a special assignment," which is accurate but tells them nothing about why it's listed. This tells you.

Fourteen readings across eight sessions. Six sessions carry one; **13.1 carries four**, which is worth knowing before you plan that week.

---

## Week 3 — Medieval and Early Modern

### 3.1: Medieval Divine Histories

**Spiegel**, "History, Historicism, and the Social Logic of the Text in the Middle Ages," *Speculum* 65, no. 1 (1990)

Sits behind Bede, the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, and Al-Biruni. The students read three medieval sources and notice they look nothing like modern history; Spiegel supplies the argument for *why that isn't a deficiency* — the chronicles are doing political work through form. This is the load-bearing claim of the session and the students never see it stated.

---

## Week 4 — Enlightenment and Scientific History

### 4.1: Enlightenment Progress

**Kant**, "Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose" (1784)

Behind Voltaire. Voltaire gives them the manners-and-commerce turn; Kant gives the directional claim — history moving toward reason and freedom — that makes "progress" a *theory* rather than a mood. Their own description says "explained in class," so this one is fully your job to deliver.

### 4.2: Scientific History — **two readings, different jobs**

**De Certeau**, *The Writing of History* (1975), Ch. 2: "The Historiographical Operation"

The institutional argument: history is produced in particular places under particular practices, and the institution decides what counts as legitimate knowledge. Pairs against Ranke's claim to disappear behind the sources.

**Bonnie Smith**, "Gender and the Practices of Scientific History," *AHR* 100, no. 4 (1995)

The *who* argument: professionalization was also a story about who was permitted to be a historian. Worth pairing explicitly with Ranke — the seminar model that produced "scientific" history also produced its exclusions. Sets up 6.2, where Scott arrives having been prepared for.

---

## Week 6 — History From Below

### 6.2: Women's and Gender History

**Butler**, *Gender Trouble* (1990), Ch. 1 (selections)

One step past Scott, who *is* assigned. Students get gender-as-category-of-analysis from Scott; Butler pushes to performativity. Flagged in the schedule as "demanding without philosophy background," so this is summary-in-lecture, not assignable — the risk is spending the session on Butler when Scott is the one they actually read.

---

## Week 7 — Microhistory

### 7.1: Microhistory

**Geertz**, "Thick Description" (1973)
- 📙 Clifford Geertz, "Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of Culture" (1973), ~25 pp. Geertz argues that culture is like a text you have to *read*, not a mechanism you can explain. Foundational for cultural history but demanding without background in anthropology; key ideas covered in lecture.
- 📗 Jill Lepore, "Historians Who Love Too Much: Reflections on Microhistory and Biography," *Journal of American History* 88, no. 1 (2001): 129–144. Lepore reflects on what microhistorians do — and the emotional and ethical complexities of writing about individual lives. Engaging and self-aware; treat it as secondary commentary on Davis and Ginzburg.

Behind Ginzburg. Students read *The Cheese and the Worms* and see a method without its justification; Geertz is the justification — culture as text to be read rather than mechanism to be explained. Also the theoretical bridge to Davis if you use her in the Historians Café menu, where "cultural historian" is a listed option.
- 📜 Natalie Zemon Davis, *The Return of Martin Guerre* (1983), selections, ~20 pp. A peasant in 16th-century France disappears and someone takes his place. Davis uses this one case to reveal an entire world. Read it like a mystery; pay attention to what kinds of evidence she uses and what she asks of them.

## Week 9 — Nations and Decolonization

### 9.2: Power, Knowledge, and the Archive — **two readings**

**Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o**, *Decolonising the Mind* (1986), Preface + Ch. 1

The linguistic dimension of colonial erasure — being made to remember in the coloniser's language. Extends Said toward something more concrete than discourse, and it's the one students find most immediately graspable if you use it.

**Chakrabarty**, *Provincializing Europe* (2000), Introduction

The structural claim that modern historical thinking is itself European-shaped. Note this is Chakrabarty's *first* appearance; he returns at 13.2 with the climate essay. Worth naming the continuity when you get there.

- 📙 Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, *Decolonising the Mind* (1986), Preface + Ch. 1. Ngũgĩ argues that colonialism's deepest damage was linguistic — forcing colonized peoples to think and remember in European languages was itself a form of historical erasure. Closely related to Said; key argument covered in lecture.
- 📙 Dipesh Chakrabarty, *Provincializing Europe* (2000), Introduction. Chakrabarty argues that all modern historical thinking has been shaped by European frameworks — the foundational postcolonial historiography argument, covered in lecture.

## Week 11 — Public History and Memory

### 11.1: History, Memory, and Public Space

**Nora**, "Between Memory and History: Les Lieux de Mémoire," *Representations* 26 (1989)

Behind Loewen, Hayden, and Trouillot Ch. 5. Nora's inversion — sites of memory appear *because* living memory is dying — is the theoretical spine of the whole session, and it makes the campus history project land differently. Students who've just been in CSWR have an instinct for this; Nora gives them the name.

---

## Week 13 — Science, Medicine, Environment

### 13.1: History of Science and Medicine — **four readings, the heaviest session in the course**

This is the session to plan first. Four advanced readings behind three student readings is more than one lecture holds; pick two.

**Kuhn**, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), Introduction + Ch. 1–2

Paradigms and their collapse. The most likely to already be half-known — students have heard "paradigm shift" without a source. Pairs directly with Shapin's "There was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution."

**Foucault**, "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971)

His most direct methodological statement: genealogy tracing how contingent, power-laden processes come to look natural. Underlies much of the second half of the course; if you only do one Foucault moment all semester, this is where it goes.

**Daston and Galison**, *Objectivity* (2007), Introduction

Objectivity as a concept with its own history — a direct callback to Ranke in Week 4. The cleanest way to show the course arguing with itself across ten weeks.

**Roy Porter**, *The Greatest Benefit to Mankind* (1997), Introduction

Medical knowledge and social power. Lecture colour more than argument; the first to cut if time is short.

### 13.2: Environmental History

**Chakrabarty**, "The Climate of History: Four Theses," *Critical Inquiry* 35, no. 2 (2009)

Humans as geological force, breaking the frameworks the course has spent a semester building. Its schedule entry says "dense but important; key theses covered in lecture" — so students see it listed and are told not to read it, which makes delivering those theses squarely your job. His second appearance in the course; the first is *Provincializing Europe* at 9.2, and naming that continuity is worth thirty seconds.
